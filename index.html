Manchester City disguised their owner's money as sponsorship to close a financial gap on the clubs above them. They should be punished for it. But the gap, and the rules that locked it in, are part of the story too.

01 / The head start · 2008–09

Before the cheating,
the gap.

When Abu Dhabi bought City in September 2008, they bought a club earning less than a third of its neighbour's income.

That season, United reported £278.5m of revenue. City reported £87m. United's advantage was £191.5m in a single season.

And the gap was growing. Between 2002–03 and 2008–09, City's revenue rose by £38m and United's by £104m. United's lead widened from £126m to £191.5m.

United built that income legitimately, over decades. City started a long way back.

A rich owner.
A much poorer club.

Source: Deloitte revenue series

02 / The rules · Break-even limits

Your income sets
your room to spend.

UEFA approved Financial Fair Play in September 2009, a year after the takeover. Its break-even rules measured spending against income and allowed only limited losses. The Premier League voted in its own version, PSR, in February 2013.

Give two clubs the same £105m loss allowance and the one earning £600m more can still spend £600m more.

Owners could cover permitted losses and pay for academies and stadiums. They could not pay for a squad big enough to close the gap. Chelsea's owner, Roman Abramovich, had been doing exactly that since 2003, before any of these rules existed. By the time he sold in 2022 he had lent the club about £1.5bn.

Protecting clubs from financial failure also protected an existing financial advantage.

Sources: UEFA and Premier League rules

03 / The disguise · 2009–10 to 2017–18

Owner money.
Sponsorship label.

By late 2009, the commission found, City had two worries: that its losses that season would break Chelsea's Premier League record of £140m, and whether it could comply with the Financial Fair Play rules then being drawn up. More money from the owner could solve neither problem, because it did not count as income.

So in early 2010 City devised a plan to dress up the owner's money as sponsorship. Over nine seasons, £830.7m came from ADUG, the owner's company, but was booked as Abu Dhabi sponsorship: 29p of every £1 City reported earning. The club's income, the commission said, was "hugely overstated".

It was not an accounting slip. The commission found City "clearly intended to circumvent the PL Rules", that some of its witnesses were dishonest, and that it tried to obstruct the investigation. City also hid payments to its manager and players.

The money was real.
The description was false.

Source: Commission decision, pp. 16–20 and 27

04 / The catch-up · 2002–03 to 2017–18

Watch the gap close.
It never shut.

Before the takeover the gap widened. After it, City's reported revenue climbed towards United's. The red area is owner money booked as sponsorship.

That money did much of the climbing. Yet in every season of the nine covered by the charges, City's reported revenue stayed below United's.

City cheated to chase the richest club in England, and still didn't catch it.

The cheating was real.
So was the head start.

05 / Where it left City · 2017–18

Closer to the top.
Still chasing United.

Without the owner money, City's 2017–18 revenue was the lowest of the six: £368.8m. With it, City reported £503.5m and were second, behind United's £589.8m.

The £134.7m of owner money booked as sponsorship that season moved City from sixth to second.

The clubs City passed, Liverpool, Chelsea, Arsenal and Spurs, paid for the cheating. United did not.

They broke the rules to close a gap.
That doesn't make the rules fair.

Subtracting the owner money is an accounting comparison, not a prediction of City's income or results without it.

Epilogue · 2024–25 revenue

Now City have
the head start.

In 2008–09 United out-earned City by 3.2 to 1. In 2024–25 City out-earned the average of the other 14 Premier League clubs by 3.3 to 1.

The rules City broke now protect City. The next club that wants to close the gap faces the same wall.

Other 14 = league total less City, United, Liverpool, Arsenal, Chelsea and Spurs, divided by 14. Derived from the revenue series below.

Accountability and a fairer competition

Catching up is no excuse for cheating.
Enforcing the rules is no reason to stop questioning them.

Punish the cheat.
Fix the game.

Angry that City cheated? Good. Aim it at the rules too.

  • Let owners invest in the open. Allow owner money paid in upfront and guaranteed, so a club can grow without hiding where the money came from.
  • Cap spending against the league, not your own history. A ceiling tied to the bottom club's TV income limits everyone equally. Clubs voted it down 7–12 in November 2025.
  • Share the money more fairly. A spending cap based on revenue turns today's income gap into a permanent one.

What these figures mean

  • Revenue is in £m. 2002–03 to 2020–21 uses Deloitte's Football Money League, with its latest restated figures. 2021–22 to 2024–25 uses its Annual Review of Football Finance club tables. City's 2016–17 accounts cover 13 months.
  • “Owner money booked as sponsorship” is the Commission's “Tagged Sums paid by ADUG” table (p. 20), covering all Abu Dhabi sponsors together. Individual sponsor allocations are redacted.
  • “City without the owner money” subtracts only those sponsorship sums from reported revenue. It is not a complete restatement of the accounts, a measure of sponsor payments alone, or an estimate of revenue or sporting results without the owner or the cheating.
  • The 29p figure divides £830.69m by City's £2,830.9m of reported revenue across 2009–10 to 2017–18, then rounds. Other figures are also rounded independently.
  • Other 14 average: (20 × league-average revenue − revenue of the six named clubs) ÷ 14. This is a mean, not the income of a typical or fixed set of clubs through time.
  • The PSR illustration uses hypothetical three-year incomes of £900m and £300m and the same £105m permitted loss. It isolates the effect of different incomes, assuming full qualifying backing and no other accounting adjustments. It is not UEFA's separate loss limit or today's squad cost ratio.
  • “Catch up” refers to the revenue gap with United, the highest earner among the six clubs in every season from 2002–03 to 2017–18. City overtook other rivals on reported revenue. Revenue comparisons cannot establish how many trophies the funding caused.
